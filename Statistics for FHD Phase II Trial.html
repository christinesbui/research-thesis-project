1. Introduction

1.1 Background

Focal hand dystonia (FHD) is a rare neurological disorder characterized by involuntary muscle contractions during skilled hand movements such as writing or playing a musical instrument. Currently, there are no disease-modifying treatments available for FHD, and symptomatic therapies, such as botulinum toxin injections, offer limited and temporary relief.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a noninvasive brain stimulation technology that regulates cortical excitability. While FDA-approved for psychiatric disorders, there has been evidence suggesting therapeutic potential for movement disorders.

Previously, our research group demonstrated that a single session of 10 Hz rTMS applied to the primary somatosensory cortex (PSC) produced clinically meaningful improvements in motor behavior in FHD patients, with effect sizes among the highest reported for TMS. These promising findings provided the rationale for the current Phase II trial examining an accelerated protocol for four rTMS sessions delivered in a single day.

1.2 Study Objectives

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the clinical benefit of four sessions of 10 Hz rTMS delivered to the PSC in a single day compared to sham stimulation in individuals with FHD.

2. Design & Methodology

2.1 Study Design

This study implemented a double-blind, randomized crossover design. Participants received both active 10 Hz rTMS and sham stimulation in random order, with a washout period between interventions. The crossover design can maximize statistical power given the rarity of FHD and can control for inter-individual variability by using each participant as their own control.

2.2 Participants

The CONSORT diagram shows a total of 55 individuals expressing interest in the study. After initial screening, 36 completed the pre-screening questions, and 30 completed the REDCap survey. Twenty-one participants enrolled in the study, including 2 healthy controls who were excluded from the treatment analysis. The final analysis sample consisted of 17 FHD participants with available kinematic writing data.

2.3 Kinematic Outcome Measures

Participants copied a holoalphabetic sentence ten times on a digital writing tablet using a pressure-sensitive pen. Kinematic data were recorded and analyzed using MovAlyzer software. For each trial, multiple kinematic features were extracted.

Based on a previous PCA analysis conducted by our group, three kinematic measures were identified as primary outcomes of interest, which were number of peak acceleration points, average velocity, and pen down duration. For the purpose of this analysis, number of peak acceleration points was designated as the primary outcome measure while average velocity and pen down duration were considered secondary outcomes.

3.4 Normality

Prior to conducting mixed effects models, we assessed distributional properties of the three key kinematic outcomes. Normality was evaluated using both histograms with overlaid density curves and calculation of skewness statistics. These assessments inform whether transformations may be necessary to meet the assumptions of linear mixed models.

Skewness values for each outcome measure were calculated, with values interpreted according to established guidelines (|skewness| < 0.5 = approximately normal; 0.5-1.0 = moderately skewed; > 1.0 = highly skewed). In contrast to the other two measures, pen down duration exhibited approximately normal distributional properties (skewness = 0.25). The histogram shows a symmetric distribution centered around 0.65-0.70, with the density curve closely approximating a normal distribution. This suggests that the proportion of time spent with pen contact during writing trials was relatively consistent across participants and trials, without the extreme values observed for the other kinematic measures.

For highly skewed variables, transformations such as natural logarithm or square root are often recommend to normalize distributions and improve model fit. However, transformations can complicate clinical interpretations of results.

To evaluate the effect of TMS on kinematic writing measures over time, we fitted linear mixed effects models with compound symmetry correlation structure for each of the three key outcomes: peak acceleration points, average velocity, and pen down duration. Each model included fixed effects for time (weeks from TMS), TMS arm (B vs. A), and their interaction (time × TMS arm), with a random intercept for each subject to account for within-subject correlation due to repeated measurements.

For peak acceleration points, the primary outcome, the model revealed that while the time × arm interaction did not achieve statistical significance, the direction of the effect suggests that Arm A may have experienced a greater reduction in peak acceleration points over time relative to Arm B. This pattern is consistent with the descriptive statistics, which showed Arm A decreasing from 341.0 at pre-TMS to 273.6 at 20 weeks, while Arm B remained relatively stable (349.8 to 340.8).

For average velocity, the significant interaction indicates that the effect of TMS on average velocity was dependent on time and treatment arm. Examination of the descriptive statistics reveals that Arm A increased from 2.47 mm/s at pre-TMS to 2.81 mm/s at 20 weeks, while Arm B decreased from 2.80 mm/s to 2.56 mm/s over the same period. This divergence suggests that the intervention received by Arm A (active TMS first, followed by sham) may have led to sustained improvements in writing speed, whereas Arm B (sham first, followed by active) did not show similar benefits. The magnitude of this effect is clinically meaningful, representing approximately a 14% increase in Arm A versus a 9% decrease in Arm B over the 20-week period.

For pen down duration, the model revealed that although the interaction reached statistical significance, the magnitude of the effect is very small (0.001 per week), representing a clinically negligible change. Over the 20-week follow-up, this would translate to a difference of approximately 0.02 between arms, which is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Both arms maintained pen down duration values between 0.66 and 0.71 throughout the study, indicating that the proportion of time spent with pen contact during writing was stable and unaffected by TMS in a clinically meaningful way.

4. Discussion

4.1 Conclusion

Overall, these findings suggest that the TMS intervention sequence received by Arm A (active first, then sham) was associated with improvements in writing kinematics, particularly increased writing velocity, compared to the reverse sequence received by Arm B. The lack of significant findings for peak acceleration points, despite descriptive trends, may reflect the high variability in this measure and the limited statistical power of this Phase II trial. These effect size estimates will be valuable for planning a adequately powered Phase III trial.

4.2 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

The small sample size, while reasonable for a Phase II trial investigating a rare disorder, it limits statistical power to detect small to moderate effect sizes. The trend-level finding for the time × arm interaction in peak acceleration points (p = 0.063) may reflect insufficient power rather than absence of a true effect. The wide confidence intervals observed for several estimates, particularly for peak acceleration points, indicate imprecision that could be addressed in a larger trial. Additionally, finidngs may not generalize well to other FHD populations.

As with any longitudinal study, participant attrition and data available occurred over the 20-week follow-up period. While mixed effects models are robust to missing data under the missing at random (MAR) assumption, we cannot rule out the possibility that dropout was related to unobserved factors or treatment response. However, attrition was balanced between arms, reducing the likelihood of systematic bias.

Age and sex were planned covariates in the statistical analysis plan but were not available in the current dataset. While these factors are unlikely to substantially influence the primary findings given the randomized design, their inclusion in future analyses would provide additional precision and control for potential confounding.

Lastly, the crossover design, while statistically efficient, introduces potential complexities including carryover effects and period effects. While a washout period was incorporated to minimize carryover, the possibility of residual effects cannot be entirely excluded.

4.3 Next Steps

The findings from this Phase II trial generate several important directions for future research.

While this current analysis focused on three key outcomes identified through PCA, the full dataset contains 22 kinematic measures that may provide additional insights into treatment effects. Future analyses will extend mixed effects modeling to all the 22 measures to identify which specific aspects of writing kinematics are most responsive to TMS.

Age and sex were planned covariates but were not available in the current dataset. Future analyses should merge the kinematic data with demographic information to adjust for potential confounding by age and sex and explore whether treatment effects differ by demographic.
